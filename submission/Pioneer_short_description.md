Team Pioneer's Stackwise answers one question: which rental housing laws apply at this address, on this date?

Tenants, landlords and housing counselors face a patchwork of statutes, ordinances, pending bills and court rulings that keep changing. A pipeline reads public law into 56 structured rules, each with a citation, a source link and the exact words it came from. Claude Sonnet 5.5 reads against a fixed schema. Code then checks that every quote is in the source and works out from the dates whether a rule is in force, not yet effective, pending or failed.

For each of 500 sample addresses, the Census geocoder gives state, county and city, and every rule is tested against year built and unit count. The answer is applies, superseded, not yet effective, pending or unknown, with a plain-English reason. When parcel data cannot say, such as a missing year built, the answer is unknown, never a guess. A stricter local law supersedes the state rule and is named. Where a state law may displace city ordinances, as with the New Jersey FAIR Act, the rule is flagged for a human reviewer, not decided.

Change tracking runs the same engine on any two dates. California's AB 325 starts on 1 January 2026 and changes answers for 250 addresses. New Jersey's FAIR Act lands in July 2027 for 140. The struck-down Massachusetts rent-control ballot question changes nothing.

A what-if panel lets users type what parcel data lacks, such as year built or unit count, and watch an unknown become a firm answer; they can also describe their own building. A date slider with Play shows answers flip the day a law starts. Everything uses public sources, every screen says it is not legal advice, and adding a city means adding its pages.
